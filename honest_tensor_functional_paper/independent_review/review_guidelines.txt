Independent review
Assume you are a senior statistician, machine learning theorist, and journal referee with extensive experience publishing and reviewing for venues such as JASA, Annals of Statistics, JRSS-B, Biometrika, Bernoulli, Statistical Science, NeurIPS, ICML, AISTATS, and COLT.
A complete draft of the research paper is now available.
Your task is to perform a deep, adversarial, end-to-end scientific review of the entire paper, similar to what would be expected from multiple expert referees at a top statistics journal.
Do not merely proofread the manuscript.
Evaluate whether the paper is:
mathematically correct;
statistically meaningful;
theoretically substantial;
genuinely novel;
internally coherent;
empirically convincing;
reproducible;

ready for submission to a strong statistics journal.

Treat the paper as if you were deciding whether to recommend acceptance, major revision, or rejection at a top-tier statistics journal.

2. Give a one-paragraph referee summary
Write a concise referee-style summary containing:
the problem;
proposed method;
main theoretical results;

This should reflect what the paper actually establishes, not merely what the Abstract claims.

3. Evaluate the statistical contribution
Determine whether the paper addresses a substantive statistical problem.
Ask:
Is the estimand scientifically/statistically meaningful?
Is the statistical problem well posed?
Is the estimator natural?
Is the setting genuinely different from existing statistical problems?
Does the contribution extend beyond a single AI/LLM application?
Is there a general statistical principle behind the work?
Would statisticians care about the theoretical problem even if the AI motivation were removed?
Distinguish:
Fundamental statistical contribution
A new theory, estimator, inferential framework, asymptotic regime, robustness result, optimality result, etc.
Technically nontrivial extension
A mathematically difficult but narrower extension of known methods.
Application-driven contribution
Existing methods applied to a new problem.
Incremental contribution
Only modest differences from existing approaches.
Explain where the paper lies on this spectrum.

4. Review the mathematical formulation
Check every major definition.
Determine whether:
notation is internally consistent;

probability spaces are clear;
dimensions match;
parameter spaces are specified;
norms are defined;
tensor/matrix dimensions are correct;
latent variables are distinguished from observed variables;
asymptotic quantities are identified;
randomness versus conditioning is clear.
Flag ambiguous statements such as:
“for sufficiently large dimensions”
without specifying which dimensions tend to infinity.
Rewrite important formulations when necessary.

7. Check theorem strength
For every main theoretical result, ask:
Is this the strongest meaningful theorem that could reasonably be proved?
For an error bound such as
∥θ^−θ⋆∥≤Crn,\|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\| \le C r_n,
evaluate:
dependence on nn;
dependence on pp;
dependence on rank rr;
sparsity ss;
contamination ϵ\epsilon;
sequence length TT;
noise;
condition numbers;
eigengaps.
Determine whether any terms appear suboptimal.
If logarithmic factors occur, ask whether they are necessary.
If a rate is claimed optimal, verify that an appropriate lower bound exists.

8. Check minimax and optimality claims
If the paper claims optimality, carefully compare upper and lower bounds.
For example, if:
sup⁡θ∈ΘEθL(θ^,θ)≲rn\sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb E_\theta L(\widehat\theta,\theta) \lesssim r_n
and
inf⁡θ~sup⁡θ∈ΘEθL(θ~,θ)≳rn,\inf_{\widetilde\theta} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} \mathbb E_\theta L(\widetilde\theta,\theta) \gtrsim r_n,
verify that:
parameter spaces match;
losses match;
assumptions match;
regimes match;
logarithmic factors are acknowledged;
the lower bound is not proved for a substantially larger or easier class.
Do not accept the word “optimal” without checking these conditions.

9. Independently audit the proofs
Treat the proofs adversarially.
For every main theorem, reconstruct the proof logic.
Break it into:
Assumptions→Lemma 1→Lemma 2→⋯→Main Result.\text{Assumptions} \rightarrow \text{Lemma 1} \rightarrow \text{Lemma 2} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \text{Main Result}.
For each step, verify:
algebra;
probability bounds;
union bounds;
expectations;
conditional expectations;
matrix inequalities;
tensor operations;
norm inequalities;
concentration inequalities;
asymptotic approximations;
stochastic-order calculations.
Do not accept statements such as:
“By standard arguments…”
unless those arguments genuinely apply.

11. Stress-test the theory
Attempt to construct counterexamples.
Ask what happens when:
signal approaches zero;
eigengap approaches zero;
rank is misspecified;
sparsity increases;

dimension grows faster;
dependence becomes stronger;
tails become heavier;
covariance becomes nearly singular;
model is misspecified.
Determine whether the theorem:
still holds;
fails gracefully;
becomes vacuous;
requires additional assumptions.
Where useful, formulate explicit counterexamples.

12. Check identifiability
Before evaluating consistency, verify that the parameter of interest is identifiable.
Ask whether
Pθ1=Pθ2P_{\theta_1} = P_{\theta_2}
can occur for
θ1≠θ2.\theta_1\neq\theta_2.
If identifiability holds only up to:
sign;
permutation;
rotation;
scale;
equivalence class,
the loss and theorem statements must reflect this.
For example, a factorization
Θ=UV⊤\Theta=UV^\top
does not identify UU and VV separately without additional constraints.
Flag any theorem estimating an inherently non-identifiable object.

21. For AI/LLM applications, audit experimental independence
If LLM-generated observations are used, examine the actual sampling structure.
For example, if multiple generations are produced from the same prompt,
Yij,j=1,…,mi,Y_{ij}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,m_i,
they may be conditionally independent given prompt XiX_i, but not marginally independent.
Check whether inference incorrectly treats all
∑imi\sum_i m_i
generations as independent observations.
Also inspect dependence across:
prompts;

layers;

decoding runs;
evaluators.
This should match the theoretical sampling model.

49. Final verdict
