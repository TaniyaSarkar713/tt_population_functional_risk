Theory review

Assume you are a senior theoretical statistician, probabilist, machine-learning theorist, and mathematical referee with expertise in asymptotic theory, high-dimensional statistics, empirical processes, concentration inequalities, random matrix theory, robust statistics, optimization, semiparametric inference, and statistical learning theory.
The manuscript already contains a set of theoretical results.
Your task is to perform a complete, adversarial mathematical audit of every theoretical result in the paper.
The goals are to:
verify that every theorem is mathematically correct;
verify that every assumption is actually sufficient;
identify assumptions that are unnecessary or stronger than needed;
determine whether stronger results can be established under weaker assumptions;
determine whether convergence rates can be sharpened;
identify missing intermediate lemmas;
produce a complete step-by-step proof of every theorem;
verify all external mathematical results used in the proofs;
search for counterexamples when assumptions are removed;
clearly distinguish proved results from plausible conjectures.
Treat the existing manuscript as a draft mathematical argument, not as authoritative truth.
Do not assume that a theorem is correct merely because it appears in the paper.
Do not preserve an assumption merely because it was used in an earlier proof.
Do not claim a stronger theorem unless you can actually prove it.
The objective is to obtain the strongest rigorously defensible version of the theory.

5. Avoid keeping assumptions merely because they simplify proofs
For each assumption, distinguish:
STRUCTURAL
Needed for identifiability or existence.
TECHNICAL BUT PROBABLY NECESSARY
Required for a central probabilistic argument.
PROOF CONVENIENCE

Prioritize removing or weakening assumptions in the last two categories.

6. Check identifiability before consistency
For every parameter or estimand, verify identifiability.
Determine whether
Pθ1=Pθ2P_{\theta_1} = P_{\theta_2}
can occur when
θ1≠θ2.\theta_1\neq\theta_2.
If the model is identifiable only up to an equivalence class,
θ1∼θ2,\theta_1\sim\theta_2,
then reformulate the loss and theorem.
Examples include:
sign;
rotation;
permutation;
scaling;
label switching.
Do not prove consistency for an unidentifiable object.

7. Re-derive every theorem independently
For each theorem:
ignore the existing proof initially;
determine what mathematical machinery should prove the result;
derive the proof independently;
compare with the manuscript proof;
reconcile differences.
This helps detect arguments that look plausible only because earlier derivations were followed mechanically.

8. Produce step-by-step proofs
For every major theorem, write a complete proof.
Do not write:
“The result follows from standard arguments.”
Instead explicitly state the argument.
A proof should have the structure:
Step 1 — Establish event
Define
En\mathcal E_n
such that
P(En)≥1−δn.P(\mathcal E_n) \ge 1-\delta_n.
Step 2 — Control stochastic term
Derive
sup⁡θ∈Θ∣(Pn−P)fθ∣≤rn.\sup_{\theta\in\Theta} |(P_n-P)f_\theta| \le r_n.
Step 3 — Use structural condition
Apply convexity, restricted eigenvalue condition, rank constraint, etc.
Step 4 — Derive deterministic inequality
For example,
c∥θ^−θ⋆∥22≤2rn∥θ^−θ⋆∥1.c\|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\|_2^2 \le 2r_n\|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\|_1.
Step 5 — Simplify
Obtain final rate.
Step 6 — Verify probability statement
Conclude
P(∥θ^−θ⋆∥≤Crn)≥1−δn.P \left( \|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\| \le Cr_n \right) \ge 1-\delta_n.
Use whatever structure is appropriate to the actual theorem.

12. Check whether independence is truly needed
If the theory assumes

ask whether independence can be replaced by:
martingale differences;

If only concentration is required, investigate appropriate dependent concentration inequalities.

13. Relax tail assumptions where possible
If the paper assumes sub-Gaussian variables, examine whether results can hold under:
Sub-exponential tails
∥X∥ψ1<∞.\|X\|_{\psi_1}<\infty.
Finite qq-th moments
E∣X∣q<∞.E|X|^q<\infty.
Finite variance
EX2<∞.E X^2<\infty.
Heavy tails with robustification
Determine how the rate changes under weaker tails.
For example, assess whether
log⁡pn\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}
becomes
(log⁡pn)1−1/q\left(\frac{\log p}{n}\right)^{1-1/q}
or another form.
Do not guess the rate; derive it.

14. Relax boundedness assumptions
If the proof assumes
∣Xi∣≤M|X_i|\le M
almost surely, determine whether this can be replaced by moment or Orlicz-norm control.
Boundedness is often stronger than necessary.
Identify which concentration inequality should replace the bounded version.

15. Relax Gaussian assumptions
If
Xi∼N(0,Σ)X_i\sim N(0,\Sigma)
is assumed, determine whether the proof really needs Gaussianity or only:
isotropy;
sub-Gaussianity;
elliptical symmetry;
finite fourth moment;
concentration of quadratic forms.
If Gaussianity is used only through a concentration inequality, seek a more general result.

16. Investigate covariance assumptions
If the theory assumes
cI⪯Σ⪯CI,cI \preceq \Sigma \preceq CI,
determine whether both bounds are required.
Could the result instead depend explicitly on
λmin⁡(Σ),λmax⁡(Σ),κ(Σ)?\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma), \qquad \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma), \qquad \kappa(\Sigma)?
A theorem with explicit condition-number dependence may be more general.

17. Investigate rank assumptions
If
rank⁡(Θ⋆)=r\operatorname{rank}(\Theta^\star)=r
is assumed exactly, determine whether the theorem can extend to approximate low rank.
For example,
∑j>rσj(Θ⋆)\sum_{j>r}\sigma_j(\Theta^\star)
may produce an approximation-error term.
Seek a result of the form
∥Θ^−Θ⋆∥F≤rn⏟estimation+ar(Θ⋆)⏟approximation.\|\widehat\Theta-\Theta^\star\|_F \le \underbrace{r_n}_{\text{estimation}} + \underbrace{a_r(\Theta^\star)}_{\text{approximation}}.
This can substantially strengthen generality.

18. Investigate exact sparsity assumptions
If
∥θ⋆∥0=s,\|\theta^\star\|_0=s,
consider approximate sparsity such as
θ⋆∈ℓq(R),0<q<1.\theta^\star\in\ell_q(R), \qquad 0<q<1.
Determine whether the result extends to an oracle inequality or approximation-error bound.

19. Investigate known structural parameters
If theory assumes known:
rank rr;
sparsity ss;
noise level σ\sigma;
contamination fraction ϵ\epsilon;
ask whether the procedure can adapt to unknown values.
A stronger result may establish adaptation without degrading the rate substantially.

20. Investigate tuning-parameter dependence
If the theorem requires
λ=Cσlog⁡pn,\lambda = C\sigma \sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}},
ask:
is CC known?
is σ\sigma known?
can λ\lambda be chosen data-adaptively?
does the theorem survive approximate tuning?
Seek results such as:
c1λn≤λ≤c2λn.c_1\lambda_n \le \lambda \le c_2\lambda_n.
This is often more practical than requiring one exact tuning value.

21. Check whether asymptotic results can become finite-sample results
If the manuscript proves only
θ^⟶pθ⋆,\widehat\theta \overset p\longrightarrow \theta^\star,
ask whether a nonasymptotic statement can be established:
P(∥θ^−θ⋆∥≤r(n,p,δ))≥1−δ.P \left( \|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\| \le r(n,p,\delta) \right) \ge 1-\delta.
Prefer explicit finite-sample results when feasible.
They can often imply consistency as a corollary.

22. Check whether consistency can be upgraded to a rate
If
θ^−θ⋆=op(1),\widehat\theta-\theta^\star=o_p(1),
determine whether the proof machinery yields
∥θ^−θ⋆∥=Op(rn).\|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\| = O_p(r_n).
Do not stop at qualitative consistency if a quantitative rate is available.

23. Check whether a rate can be upgraded to asymptotic distribution
If
ψ^−ψ⋆=Op(n−1/2),\widehat\psi-\psi^\star = O_p(n^{-1/2}),
investigate whether
n(ψ^−ψ⋆)⟶dN(0,V).\sqrt n (\widehat\psi-\psi^\star) \overset d\longrightarrow N(0,V).
Identify:
linear expansion;
influence function;
stochastic remainder;
variance estimator.
If feasible, derive:
ψ^−ψ⋆=1n∑i=1nϕ(Zi)+op(n−1/2).\widehat\psi-\psi^\star = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(Z_i) + o_p(n^{-1/2}).
24. Check whether asymptotic normality can be made uniform
Instead of only pointwise
n(ψ^−ψ)⇒N(0,V),\sqrt n (\widehat\psi-\psi) \Rightarrow N(0,V),
ask whether a uniform result holds over
P∈P.P\in\mathcal P.
For example,
sup⁡P∈Psup⁡t∣P(n(ψ^−ψ(P))σ^≤t)−Φ(t)∣→0.\sup_{P\in\mathcal P} \sup_t \left| P \left( \frac{\sqrt n(\widehat\psi-\psi(P))} {\widehat\sigma} \le t \right) -\Phi(t) \right| \to0.
A uniform result is considerably stronger for inference.

25. Investigate Berry–Esseen-type bounds
If asymptotic normality is proved, determine whether finite-sample normal approximation can be bounded:
sup⁡t∣P(Zn≤t)−Φ(t)∣≤Crn.\sup_t \left| P(Z_n\le t)-\Phi(t) \right| \le C r_n.
This can strengthen the inferential contribution.

26. Investigate confidence intervals and testing
If asymptotic normality exists, determine whether the paper can derive:
CI1−α=ψ^±z1−α/2σ^n.CI_{1-\alpha} = \widehat\psi \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\widehat\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
Prove coverage:
P{ψ⋆∈CI1−α}→1−α.P \{ \psi^\star\in CI_{1-\alpha} \} \to 1-\alpha.
If feasible, derive hypothesis tests and local power.

27. Investigate minimax optimality
For every estimator rate
rn,r_n,
ask whether the rate is optimal over the relevant parameter class.
Seek a lower bound:
inf⁡θ~sup⁡θ∈ΘEθL(θ~,θ)≥crn.\inf_{\widetilde\theta} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta} E_\theta L(\widetilde\theta,\theta) \ge c r_n.
Use:
Fano;
Le Cam;
Assouad;
testing reductions;
packing arguments.
Compare the lower bound to the estimator's upper bound.

28. Verify parameter classes in minimax arguments
Ensure the lower-bound class is the same as—or appropriately nested relative to—the upper-bound class.
Do not claim minimax optimality if:
lower bound uses a larger parameter class;
loss differs;
rank differs;
noise assumptions differ;
contamination model differs.

29. Investigate oracle inequalities
If the method uses structural regularization, consider proving
R(θ^)−R(θ⋆)≤Cinf⁡θ∈Θ{R(θ)−R(θ⋆)+pen⁡(θ)}.R(\widehat\theta)-R(\theta^\star) \le C \inf_{\theta\in\Theta} \left\{ R(\theta)-R(\theta^\star) + \operatorname{pen}(\theta) \right\}.
An oracle inequality may provide stronger adaptivity than a single fixed-class result.

30. Investigate misspecification
Do not assume the model is exactly correct unless necessary.
If
P0∉{Pθ:θ∈Θ},P_0\notin \{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\},
define a pseudo-true target
θ†=arg⁡min⁡θ∈ΘEP0ℓ(θ;Z).\theta^\dagger = \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta} E_{P_0}\ell(\theta;Z).
Determine whether consistency/rates can be proved for
θ^→θ†.\widehat\theta\to\theta^\dagger.
This can make the theory much more robust.

31. Investigate contamination robustness
If observations follow
Pϵ=(1−ϵ)Pθ⋆+ϵQ,P_\epsilon = (1-\epsilon)P_{\theta^\star} + \epsilon Q,
derive explicit dependence on ϵ\epsilon.
Seek bounds such as
∥θ^−θ⋆∥≲rn+ϵα.\|\widehat\theta-\theta^\star\| \lesssim r_n+\epsilon^\alpha.
Determine whether the contamination term is minimax optimal.

32. Investigate adversarial versus stochastic contamination
If the theory handles random outliers, determine whether it extends to adversarial contamination.
Clearly distinguish:
Huber contamination\text{Huber contamination}
from
deterministic adversarial corruption.\text{deterministic adversarial corruption}.
Do not conflate them.

33. Investigate dependence on condition numbers and eigengaps
If bounds contain

34. Check dimensional regimes
If the theorem assumes
p=o(n),p=o(n),

p/n→γ∈(0,∞),p/n\to\gamma\in(0,\infty),

p≫n.p\gg n.

If the obstruction is only matrix invertibility, investigate regularized alternatives.

35. Check whether logarithmic factors can be removed
Suppose the rate is
slog⁡pn.\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{n}}.
Ask whether the log⁡p\log p term is fundamental or proof-induced.
Inspect:
union bounds;

Try sharper empirical-process or chaining arguments.

36. Check whether union bounds are unnecessarily crude
Whenever a proof uses
P(⋃j=1pAj)≤∑j=1pP(Aj),P \left( \bigcup_{j=1}^p A_j \right) \le \sum_{j=1}^pP(A_j),

37. Check covering-number calculations
If the proof uses

verify the covering-number bound carefully.
Check:
metric;
radius;
dimensional dependence;
compactness;
constants.
Do not import a covering bound for the wrong norm.

38. Check empirical-process arguments
If using
sup⁡f∈F∣(Pn−P)f∣,\sup_{f\in\mathcal F} |(P_n-P)f|,
verify:
measurability;
envelope;
entropy;
stochastic equicontinuity;
symmetrization conditions.
Determine whether Donsker assumptions are really needed or whether modern high-dimensional Gaussian approximation is more suitable.

39. Check stochastic equicontinuity
Whenever a proof replaces
θ^\widehat\theta
with
θ⋆\theta^\star
inside a stochastic process, verify that the replacement is justified uniformly.
Do not infer uniform behavior from pointwise convergence.

40. Check Taylor expansions carefully
For expansions such as
∇Ln(θ^)=∇Ln(θ⋆)+∇2Ln(θ~)(θ^−θ⋆),\nabla L_n(\widehat\theta) = \nabla L_n(\theta^\star) + \nabla^2L_n(\widetilde\theta) (\widehat\theta-\theta^\star),

If using higher-order expansions, bound every remainder.

41. Check matrix perturbation arguments
For eigenvector/subspace results, verify use of:
Weyl;
Davis–Kahan;
Wedin;
sin-Θ\Theta bounds.
Explicitly define eigengap.
For example,
Δ=λr(M)−λr+1(M).\Delta = \lambda_r(M)-\lambda_{r+1}(M).

42. Check tensor perturbation arguments
For tensor decompositions, verify:
identifiability;
uniqueness;

Do not assume matrix perturbation results transfer directly to tensors.

43. Check optimization-statistics interaction
If the estimator is defined by a nonconvex objective, separate:
Statistical result
Property of global minimizer.
Computational result
Whether the algorithm reaches it.
If only a stationary point is guaranteed, determine whether the statistical theorem applies to stationary points.
A stronger paper may need a joint statistical-computational guarantee.

44. Investigate computational-statistical tradeoffs
Ask whether achieving the statistical optimum requires computationally infeasible optimization.
If so, characterize:
statistically optimal rate\text{statistically optimal rate}
versus
polynomial-time achievable rate.\text{polynomial-time achievable rate}.
This may itself be a theoretical contribution.

45. Check initialization dependence
If a computational theorem requires
∥θ(0)−θ⋆∥≤R,\|\theta^{(0)}-\theta^\star\| \le R,

An oracle initialization is not a practical guarantee.

46. Audit probability statements
Check distinctions among:

48. Audit constants
Whenever a theorem states

49. Check edge and boundary cases
Test theorem statements at boundaries.
Examples:

50. Compare with known special cases
If the proposed theory generalizes known work, verify that under special assumptions it recovers the classical result.
For example:
ϵ=0\epsilon=0
should reduce a robust estimator bound to the uncontaminated case.
This is an important sanity check.

51. Search actively for counterexamples
For every weakened assumption, attempt to find a failure example.

52. Distinguish sufficient from necessary assumptions
The theorem may require

Explicitly classify assumptions as:

53. Search for weaker theorem formulations
Sometimes a strong target result requires restrictive assumptions, while a weaker but useful result holds much more generally.
For example:
Strong result
n(θ^−θ⋆)⇒N(0,V)\sqrt n (\widehat\theta-\theta^\star) \Rightarrow N(0,V)
under strong conditions.
General result
θ^→pθ⋆\widehat\theta \to_p \theta^\star
under much weaker conditions.
Consider presenting both.

54. Build a hierarchy of results
Where possible organize theory as:
Theorem 1 — Identification
Theorem 2 — Consistency under weak assumptions
Theorem 3 — Nonasymptotic rate under stronger assumptions
Theorem 4 — Asymptotic normality
Theorem 5 — Minimax lower bound
Corollary — Valid confidence interval
This makes assumption/result tradeoffs transparent.

55. Investigate whether results can hold uniformly over model classes
Instead of a single fixed parameter θ⋆\theta^\star, seek:
sup⁡θ∈ΘnPθ(L(θ^,θ)>Crn)→0.\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_n} P_\theta \left( L(\widehat\theta,\theta)>Cr_n \right) \to0.
Uniform theory is generally stronger than pointwise theory.

56. Investigate adaptivity
If the optimal rate depends on unknown smoothness/rank/sparsity, determine whether the method adapts.
For example,
rn(s)r_n(s)
with unknown ss.
Can a data-driven procedure achieve
rn(s)r_n(s)
simultaneously over a range of ss?

57. Investigate robustness–efficiency tradeoffs
For robust estimators, determine whether stronger robustness reduces efficiency under the clean model.
Quantify both if possible.
For example:
ARE⁡=VefficientVrobust.\operatorname{ARE} = \frac{V_{\text{efficient}}} {V_{\text{robust}}}.
This can strengthen interpretation.

58. Check semiparametric efficiency where relevant
If estimating a low-dimensional functional
ψ(P),\psi(P),
investigate:
tangent space;
influence function;
efficient influence function;
semiparametric efficiency bound.
Determine whether the proposed estimator attains that bound.

59. Check bootstrap validity where relevant
If bootstrap inference is used or proposed, prove its validity.
For example:
sup⁡t∣P∗(Tn∗≤t)−P(Tn≤t)∣→p0.\sup_t \left| P^\ast(T_n^\ast\le t) - P(T_n\le t) \right| \to_p0.
Do not assume bootstrap validity automatically in high-dimensional or nonregular problems.

60. Investigate nonregular cases
Ask whether:
parameter lies on boundary;
eigengap can vanish;
rank changes;
support changes;
nuisance is unidentified under null.
Standard asymptotic normality may fail.
Identify such cases explicitly.

61. Check whether theorem conditions are observable
Distinguish assumptions involving unknown theoretical quantities from conditions practitioners can diagnose.
If an assumption is not testable, explain its theoretical role and practical limitation.

62. Compare assumptions with closest literature
For each major competing theorem, build:

63. Explicitly search for Pareto improvements
A truly stronger theorem might simultaneously achieve:
weaker assumptions+same rate\text{weaker assumptions} + \text{same rate}
or
same assumptions+better rate.\text{same assumptions} + \text{better rate}.
But sometimes improvement in one dimension requires sacrifice in another.
Clearly state the tradeoff.

64. Do not over-strengthen results
If weakening an assumption worsens the rate, report both versions.
For example:
Strong-tail theorem
rn(1).r_n^{(1)}.
Weak-moment theorem
rn(2)>rn(1).r_n^{(2)}>r_n^{(1)}.
This is preferable to pretending one theorem dominates in every regime.

65. Produce proof sketches before full proofs
For each theorem, first give a short proof roadmap:
main decomposition;
core stochastic bound;
deterministic argument;
conclusion.
Then provide the complete proof.
This makes hidden logical gaps easier to identify.

66. Write every lemma needed for completeness
If a proof depends on a result not sufficiently standard or not directly available from the literature, state and prove a new lemma.
Do not bury essential mathematical steps inside paragraphs.

67. Verify every lemma independently
A lemma is not automatically correct because it is “technical.”
For each lemma:
check assumptions;
derive proof;
test edge cases;
inspect constants;
identify dependencies.

68. Verify corollaries truly follow
For every corollary, explicitly substitute the additional assumptions into the parent theorem.
Do not state a corollary that requires another hidden argument.

69. Check theorem numbering and logical order
Order results so that prerequisites appear before dependent results.
A reader should not need a later lemma to understand an earlier theorem unless clearly indicated.

70. Check proof modularity
If the same probabilistic bound is used repeatedly, isolate it as a lemma.
Avoid reproving similar results inconsistently in multiple places.

71. Produce a theorem-strengthening matrix

Final principle
The objective is not to defend the theory already written in the manuscript.
The objective is to determine the strongest theorem that can actually be proved.
For every result ask:
Is it correct?Are all assumptions necessary?Can the assumptions be weakened?Can the conclusion be strengthened?Is the rate sharp?Can optimality be established?Does the proof contain any hidden gap?\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Is it correct?}\\[2mm] \text{Are all assumptions necessary?}\\[2mm] \text{Can the assumptions be weakened?}\\[2mm] \text{Can the conclusion be strengthened?}\\[2mm] \text{Is the rate sharp?}\\[2mm] \text{Can optimality be established?}\\[2mm] \text{Does the proof contain any hidden gap?} \end{array} }
The preferred outcome is not necessarily the theorem with the most impressive statement.
The preferred outcome is the strongest mathematically correct, fully proved, transparent, and defensible result that can survive scrutiny from an expert theoretical-statistics referee.
Never upgrade a conjecture into a theorem merely because it appears plausible.
Never preserve a stronger rate or weaker assumption unless the proof actually supports it.
